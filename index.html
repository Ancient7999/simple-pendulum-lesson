Acceleration due to gravity with a simple pendulum

Aim

To determine the acceleration due to gravity, g, using a simple pendulum.

Learning outcomes

By the end of this session, students should be able to:

  • Determine the acceleration due to gravity using a simple pendulum.
  • Record the quantity that depends on gravity: the period of oscillation, and how it changes with length.
  • Represent data graphically and analyse experimental error.

Why it matters in medicine

Periodic motion in the body

A swinging leg during walking behaves much like a pendulum. Its natural period helps explain a comfortable walking pace.

Rehabilitation timing

Therapists use steady rhythms, sometimes paced by a pendulum or metronome, to time repetitive exercises.

Biological rhythms

Heartbeat, breathing and gait are repeating cycles. The ideas of period and frequency used here are how we describe them.

Theory

A simple pendulum is a small, heavy bob hanging from a fixed point on a light, inextensible string. Pulled a little to one side and released, it swings to and fro about its lowest point, the mean position.

Length L

From the point of suspension to the centre of the bob.

One oscillation

A full to-and-fro swing: from one side, across to the other, and back again.

Period T

The time for one oscillation, in seconds.

Amplitude

How far the bob is pulled aside. Keep the angle small, under 10°.

The forces on the bob

The weight mg splits into two parts. Along the string, mg cos θ is balanced by the tension T. Along the arc, mg sin θ pulls the bob back towards the mean position: this is the restoring force.

From the force to the formula

  1. For a small angle, sin θ ≈ θ (in radians), and θ = x / L, where x is the distance along the arc. So the restoring force isF = −mg sin θ ≈ −(mg / L) x
  2. Dividing by m gives the acceleration:a = −(g / L) xAcceleration proportional to displacement and directed back to the centre: this is simple harmonic motion, with ω² = g / L.
  3. The period of SHM is T = 2π / ω, soT = 2π √(L / g)
  4. Square both sides:T² = (4π² / g) L
Small angles only

T = 2π√(L/g) holds only for small angles, under about 10°. At larger angles the true period is longer than the formula predicts: at 30° it is about 1.7% longer, and a g calculated from such a period comes out about 3.5% too low.

T² = (4π² / g) · L
y = S · x

Plot T² on the y-axis against L on the x-axis: the points lie on a straight line through the origin. Its slope is S = 4π² / g, so

g = 4π² / S

Steps

Good practice
  • Small angle. T = 2π√(L/g) holds only for small angles, under about 10°. At 30° the period is about 1.7% longer, which shifts the value of g.
  • Measure to the centre of the bob. L = string length (from the clamp) + radius of the bob.
  • Time at the mean position. The bob moves fastest there, so the moment it passes is sharpest. Start the watch as it passes and count "zero", then count each return in the same direction.
  • Swing in one plane. Release gently without pushing, so the bob does not trace an ellipse.
  • Time many oscillations. The reaction error (about 0.2 s) is shared over all of them. Over 10 oscillations it is 0.02 s per period; over 20, only 0.01 s.

Real readings scatter more than this example. Periods timed by hand over only 10 oscillations can shift the result, and a timing error matters most at short lengths: at L = 20 cm, being 0.2 s off on T10 changes T² by about 4.5%, while at 80 cm the same slip changes it by about 2%.

Common slips

Forgetting ÷ 10

T10 is the time for ten swings. Squaring 12.69 instead of 1.269 gives 161 instead of 1.61: every T² is 100 times too big, and g comes out 100 times too small.

Slope upside down

T² is on the y-axis, so S = ΔT² / ΔL. Taking ΔL / ΔT² = 60 / 2.416 = 24.8 would give g = 1.59, which is far off.

Mixing units

With L in cm the answer is in cm/s². Comparing 980 with 9.8 directly mixes cm and m. Divide by 100 first, or compare with 980 cm/s².

Plotting T, not T²

T against L is a curve. Only T² against L is a straight line whose slope is 4π² / g.
